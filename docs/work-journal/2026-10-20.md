# 2026-10-20

## A probe's read error, and a sector the table read as well

The day opened on the standup's NEXT item: fsd's probe of a partition took a
failed read for "no filesystem here", so `mount -a` swapped a USB stick in at
`/` past a disk that would not read, and `format` went on to format the
partition it could not read. The fix was small, the probe answering the
read's I/O error and `vfs::first_mounting` keeping the first one, but the rig
was not. My first `make test-probe-ioerr` failed one sector of the disk
through QEMU's blkdebug and passed with main's rule put back. The sector was
LBA 1, which the partition table's GPT check reads as well as the probe, so
the error stopped the boot before any probe saw it, on both trees alike. The
rig moved to an exFAT-only disk with its root directory sector failing, a
sector only the probe reads, and the control then failed as it should. A
single boot could not tell `format`'s refusal from the stick swap, so each got
a boot of its own.

The review of #329 found three things the first fix had left. The probe
answered at its first arm's error, so ext2, whose superblock is not the
sector FAT32 and exFAT read, was never tried past a bad first sector. `format`
went on to the next candidate past one that would not read, which with the
boot partition unknown was the ESP. And `mount N` still said "no filesystem".
One more came from the rig itself: fsd's "mount failed" line was three
console writes, and accountd's boot line landed inside it often enough to
make a check flaky. It is one write now.

## A volume as long as its partition, over five rounds

The review of #329 also found that a stale exFAT boot sector, its root past
the end of the device, now made `format` refuse a disk it could have taken.
The roadmap's `cluster_end` item said the same thing from the other side:
every filesystem took its volume's size from its own boot sector, so one
claiming more than its partition put the next partition's sectors inside the
volume. #330 gave each `mount_at` the most the volume may span,
`vfs::volume_bound`, and refused a boot sector claiming more.

Five review rounds ran against it, and the bound was not the hard part. The
first two (ten findings each, all taken) cut the bound to the disk, to sector
2^32 and to the next partition's start, and found that a volume can claim no
more than its partition and still not fit inside its own claim: exFAT's heap
or FAT outside its volume, FAT32 with no FAT or no reserved sector, ext2 with
a block-size shift past what a u32 holds. Each rule got a shape case on the
host model in `test-volume-bound.py`, which confirms a case breaks exactly
the one rule meant before anything boots. That model earned its place twice:
after the third round pinned ext2's first data block to the superblock's
own, it caught the descriptor-table case breaking two rules, and the only
shape left that reaches that rule turned out to be a two-block volume.

The controls had their own wrong turns. The length rule's control passed
the first time because `mount_partition` had a second call site and the
mutation reached one. ext2's first-block rule cannot break alone (the
descriptor table starts after the first data block, so the table rule breaks
with it), so its control cannot fail, and it stays, documented, to guard a
subtraction. The block-size case first used a shift of 34, which a release
build masks to 2 and the length rule then refuses; 32 masks to 0 and only the
shift rule sees it. Two more rules, FAT32's sum order and exFAT's sector
shift, matter only with overflow checks on, and are written down as such.

The fourth round, scoped to the third's commits, found the one real bug of
the later rounds: ext2 checked only that `s_inode_size` was not 0, and a
64-byte inode panicked fsd on its first write. The fifth, a low review of
that one commit, read only the diff in six seconds and found nothing; I
checked by hand what it should have asked, whether anything in fsd assumes
an inode of at most 256 bytes, before saying the PR was done.

## ext2's block numbers, and a free path that kept stopping halfway

With the volume bounded, ext2's `s_blocks_count` was a bound worth having,
and #331 put every sector the mounted arm reads or writes through one
`Volume` that refuses a sector past it. An inode number past the count is
refused the same way, and the allocators stop at the last group's last block
and inode. An MBR holds four partitions and the rig needed the root and three
damaged volumes, so the witness is unpartitioned space at the disk's end,
right after the last volume, and the firmware boots from NVMe as in
`test-volume-bound`. Five controls failed as they should on the first run,
the allocator's block cap among them, though with `Volume` in place that cap
only turns an I/O error into "disk full".

Four review rounds followed, and each found the same shape of defect in a
new place. A bound that refuses turns a silent wrong write into an error,
and an error is only safe where the operation can still stop. ext2's free
path runs after its commit point: `rm` has removed the directory entry, an
overwrite has written the new inode. The first round found `rm` of a file
whose indirect pointer lay past the volume failing there, leaving the inode
allocated and half freed, and the fix taught the free path to read no
pointer block past the volume. The second found the same through a bitmap
past the volume, which my own doc comment had just said could not happen,
and found the free counts raised for a bit already clear. The third found a
damaged entry that could be listed but never removed, the free path holding
three 4 KiB buffers on fsd's 56 KiB stack, and `rm` of a fast symlink freeing
the blocks its target's text named. My fix for that one, "an inode with
`i_blocks` 0 frees nothing", was wrong both ways, and the fourth round said
so: an attribute block raises `i_blocks` on a fast symlink, and damage can
zero it on a regular file. A fast symlink is now told by its type and a
length under 60, as Linux makes one. That round also showed no rig had ever
freed a double-indirect file, after the walk had been rewritten twice;
BIGD, 270 KiB, now must be freed whole, 273 blocks.

The instruments slipped too. The first OWI check failed on correct code: it
expected `x\n`, and `echo` writes CR LF. With the bound taken out, a block
the allocator took from a "bitmap" in the zeroed witness was block 1 of C,
its superblock, and the host's reader crashed on the zeroed volume instead of
reporting; the verdicts now say a volume is gone. One control boot timed out
at the login prompt and failed every check, which proved nothing until it was
rerun alone. And twice I wrote something down before checking it: a PR
comment saying 37 checks when the rig runs 35, and three comments whose commit
lists came from `git log -5` rather than from the round's own commits.

What the rounds set aside is one roadmap item, written with Linux's checks
to follow: a block number inside the volume but in its metadata
(`ext2_check_descriptors`, `ext2_data_block_valid`), the triple-indirect tree
and attribute block the free path never frees, and the whole-block bitmap
rewrite. `ensure_block`'s two allocation-order leaks are another. Both are
older than #331.

## The day's shape

Three pull requests, #329 to #331, and ten review rounds: one on the first,
five on the second, four on the third. They close one question for every
format fsd mounts: nothing it reads or writes lies past its volume, whatever
the boot sector or a block pointer says. The rounds did not converge the way
#330's did. On #331 the bound itself drew findings only in the first round;
every later one was in what a refusal does to a path that cannot stop, or in
code older than the branch. The suite stood at 238 host tests all day, and
the stale `test-listing-walk` figures in CLAUDE.md (70, 74 and 126 sectors,
now 63, 64 and 121) were corrected at the closeout.
