# 2026-09-22

## Async remote mount, step 2: the held fid session onto the parked engine

Yesterday ended with step 2 sized from the code it replaces rather than from its
one line in the plan, eight findings written down before any of it existed.
Today was writing it, and the sizing held: a day, the shape of step 1, deleting
more than it adds, no ABI change but one to the loader. Every one of the eight
findings landed as written, and the one thing the sizing deliberately did not
pin down (the stack) is the one thing that needed a boot to settle.

**What it is.** A remote fid verb used to run a blocking TCP round trip on
netd's serve frame: `handle_rmount` routed it to `session_rmount`, which opened
or found a `ClientSession`, did the signed exchange, and only then returned a
reply. Now it parks, exactly as a path verb has since step 1. The held session
is a `SessionConn`, the same `Wire` engine as the path table with one added
const buffer, `held`, that carries the first verb while its `NP_SESSION` opens.
`handle_rmount`'s fid branch calls `park_session` and returns without waiting;
`service_remotes`, now run once per table, does the two-phase open (sign the
held verb only after the far side answers the `NP_SESSION` with status 0),
delivers replies to the caller's task identity, keeps the fid refcount, and
closes a session at zero fids or on a fail-dead reply. The whole synchronous
client-session path is gone: `ClientSession`, `TcpScratch`, `client_connect`,
`client_exchange`, `client_close`, `session_exchange`, `find_or_open_session`,
`session_rmount`, `reap_client_sessions`. Net 462 lines out, 339 in.

**The two-phase park was the load-bearing finding.** The far export serves one
framed request per session reset and does not pipeline two frames on the stream,
so a fresh session cannot send the `NP_SESSION` and the verb back to back. The
verb has to wait, raw and unsigned, until the session is accepted, then be
signed from the service pass. Signing there is affordable only because
`service_remotes` runs at the top level of `serve`, never from the re-entrant
`cpu`-run drain, which is the depth that made parking there over-reach a day
ago. The `slen == 0` guard before phase-two signing is the small correctness
point: TCP has acked the `NP_SESSION` out of the send buffer by the time its
reply arrives, so signing the held verb over that buffer cannot corrupt a
request still in flight.

**The overlap refusal is now a real, exercised limit.** The blocking path made
two fid verbs from one user to one endpoint impossible to overlap: the first
held the event loop until it answered. Parked, they can, and `park_session`
refuses the second `FS_ERR_BUSY`. libc's `read()` loop does not retry on it, so
two C programs sharing one remote mount under one user can see a read fail busy.
That is a documented boundary, not a bug, until a queue is worth its stack.

## The stack moved, and only a boot showed how far

Finding 5 of the sizing said the stack moves rather than shrinks, and that it
should be measured on the rig, not reasoned. It was right to say so. A
`SessionConn` is 2896 bytes (measured through a compile error: its send and
receive buffers plus the 752-byte held verb); the old `ClientSession` held no
buffers at all, a round trip borrowing the caller's. Three session slots
resident on `serve`'s frame is ~8.5 KB more, and it sits under the deep
`cpu`-run chain (`handle_run` then `tcp_run`) that is already at the guard page,
the exact chain #147's re-entrant refusal exists for.

The two-node ext2 rig faulted `tcp_run`'s own prologue: `esr_el1=0x9200004f`,
`far_el1` inside the guard page, a clean EL0 fault, netd restarted. Symbolizing
the `elr` put it at `tcp_run + 0x0`, and the fault address was 704 bytes past
the 48 KB stack top into the guard. So the overflow was small, which matches
step 1 having left ~7 KB of headroom that step 2's resident table just ate.
Grew `STACK_PAGES` 12 to 14, restoring that headroom, and the rig went green
both nodes. Step 3 removes the re-entrant run drain and can hand the pages back.

The lesson is the one the async arc keeps teaching from a new angle. This was
not the plan's "should fit 48 KB" being careless. It was the reason the plan
said measure: the compiler decides where the resident table lands, and it
landed on a chain already at the edge. The single variable I could not read off
the source was the one that needed the boot.

## Checks, each with a control that was measured

The async rig gained two checks. Check 5: a C program's `open`/`fstat`/`read`/
`close` (`/bin/cremote`) is served from a parked session slot held `Established`
between the verbs; its control is a close-after-every-reply mutation in
`service_remotes`, which makes the `fstat` reach a clunked fid and fail,
measured on the mutated image. Check 6: a fid verb parked on a peer whose
`NP_SESSION` reply never comes does not stall a concurrent path-verb `cat` of a
live peer, the cat's bytes landing before the open fails at the deadline, which
is the step-1/step-2 latency boundary the third review of #149 named; its
control is `main`'s netd, where the session path still blocks the loop and the
cat is served after the failure, measured. The two-node ext2 witnesses passed
with zero faults both nodes: `cbig` reads back byte-identical, `cwrite` as root
writes through a remote fid, `cwrite` as user is refused by permission. And
`make test-reentrant-session` is served on the larger stack, both recipes
refused with no fault.

One driver detail worth keeping: check 6's `exec /bin/cremote` gives the prompt
back before cremote's first (local) read line prints, so the next step waits on
that line, `SH.BIN`, not on the bare prompt, or the cat is typed into cremote's
still-running output. The rig's freshness guard and its per-node fault count are
what caught the stack overflow as a real fault rather than a flake.

## Step 3: the run parks, the drain goes, and a latent bug turns out to be live

Step 2 landed in the morning, so the user asked for step 3: remove `tcp_run`
and the re-entrant drain. It is the smallest of the three arcs to describe and
the one that found the most.

`handle_run` became `park_run`. It still builds and signs the `NP_RUN`, then
parks it and returns. `service_remotes` drains the slot's receive buffer into
`PendingRun` as output arrives, which is what keeps the slot's buffer small,
and answers the caller on the peer's FIN. A run slot is identified by
`verb == NP_RUN`, which conveniently also encodes the two things that make a
run different: its reply is a stream rather than a framed NP reply, and it
carries no signature. No new field was needed. Out went `tcp_run`, `pump_conns`
(which existed only so `tcp_run` could keep the export flowing), the re-entrant
drain, the by-sender refusal of #147, the chained-cpu refusal, the
`Option<&mut PendingRun>` whose only job was to mean "this is the re-entrant
drain", and `handle_client`'s `conns` parameter. 266 lines out, 144 in.

**The latent overflow was not latent.** The plan has called it that since it
was written: the cpu child's Phase 4b `/host` reads take the deep path from
inside the run and are not refused, because they *are* the run. I went to
verify that step 3 closed it and ran the control on `main` first. Both
`cpu <A> ls /host` and `cpu <A> cat /host/HELLO.TXT` fault netd at the guard
page and the supervisor restarts the server. Every time, not intermittently.
Phase 4b remote-fs, the headline of the cpu model, was broken on `main` and
nothing had reported it because no rig ever ran a `/host` read. On the step-3
branch both return the right bytes with zero faults. That is the single most
valuable thing this step produced, and it came from insisting on a control for
a claim I could have just asserted.

## Two measurements that contradicted me

**The stack does not come back.** The plan said step 3 would hand back the two
pages step 2 took, and `tcp_run`'s ~4 KB frame did leave the tree. At 48 KB
netd still faults, 1936 bytes into the guard page, in `caller_name` beneath
`park_run`. Deleting the deepest function did not make the deepest chain
shallower, because what dominates is no longer a transient frame at all: it is
step 2's resident tables on `serve`'s frame, which every chain stands on.

I then tried twice to buy the page back and both attempts were refused by
measurement. `#[inline(never)]` on `park_run` made it *worse*, 3072 bytes over
instead of 1936, because inlining had been letting the compiler overlap its
buffers with `handle_client`'s fetch and resolve buffers, whose lifetimes are
disjoint from the run's. That is the step-1 lesson pointing the other way, and
the distinction is now written down: the attribute is about SIBLING branches
that would otherwise both sit in one frame, not about call depth in general.
Sharing one reply buffer in `service_remotes` was the second attempt and moved
this chain not at all. Fourteen pages stays, with the reason recorded next to
the constant so the next person does not re-derive it.

**The inverted rig lost a distinction, and saying so is part of the change.**
`test-reentrant-session.sh` asserted a refusal; step 3 makes every recipe
served, so the assertions flip. But while the answer was a refusal, "refused"
and "succeeded" were different strings, so the harness could tell whether the
condition had been created and retry when the run had returned first. Now
served-during-the-run and served-after-the-run-returned are the same bytes.
The rig is a regression check against the refusal and the overflow, not a proof
of overlap, and its header says exactly that. The async rig's ordering checks
are where overlap is actually proven. It also gained the `cbig` recipe, which
the by-sender refusal had made too flaky to grade, and its `resolve` recipe now
expects "no response" because the two-node link has no SLIRP and therefore no
DNS. That last one I got wrong first and the rig failed four times for the
right reason: I had asserted a successful lookup on a rig that cannot resolve
anything.

## The environment that never arrived

The last thing the day's reviews left was a defect the #153 reviewer found
while checking a table row I had written: `ENV_STAGE` publishes a 2048-byte
maximum and enforced 512. Every staging call ran the blanket `valid_user_range`
first, and for argv, cwd and namespaces that was merely redundant, since their
own limits are 512, 128 and 256. The environment's own check was dead above
512.

What made it worth fixing rather than noting was the shape of the failure. The
environment travels as ONE blob, so a larger one is refused outright and the
child inherits nothing, not a truncated set. And the shell discarded the
return value, three lines below two siblings that both check theirs. So the
whole environment vanished without a word.

I measured it before believing it. On `main`, with five variables set, a
spawned `printenv` printed no variables at all, not even `PATH`. On the fixed
tree it prints all six. That is the kind of control that turns "the reviewer
says the bound is wrong" into "here is the bug".

The fix the user picked is the one that makes the constants true: a
`valid_stage_range` that checks each staging call against its own maximum,
with containment in the caller's region unchanged, since that is the check
that actually carries the safety. The precedent was already in the file, three
lines up: `valid_msg_range` does exactly this for messages at 768, and its
comment even says the staging buffers "still use" the 512 check, which is the
bug stated as a fact and not noticed.

## A report I said could not fire, and the review showing it could

The shell now reports both a refusal and a `serialize` truncation. I went
looking for how to exercise the truncation branch, decided it was unreachable
through typed input, exercised it by temporarily lowering `ENV_MAX` to 256
instead, and wrote that conclusion into this journal. A review of the change
took the claim apart, and it was wrong.

My arithmetic assumed a value bounded by `ENV_VALUE_SIZE` and a short name.
The real bound is the 128-byte input LINE, which covers the name and the value
together, so an entry is at most 128 bytes whatever the split: `set` plus a
23-character name plus `=` plus a 100-character value is exactly 128
characters, and serializes to exactly 128 bytes. Sixteen of those are 2052
bytes against an `ENV_MAX` of 2048. It overflows by four. `syscall-abi`'s own
doc comment had said so in plain words the whole time, that a maximally-full
environment "truncates, dropping trailing vars", and I read past it while
computing a number that disagreed.

So I drove it for real, no mutation: fourteen variables plus overwritten
`HOME` and `PATH`, then `/bin/printenv` by absolute path because overwriting
`PATH` is what the last of the sixteen entries costs. It printed "environment
too large to export in full (15 of 16 variables passed)" and the child received
exactly fifteen. The mutation control is gone from the record; a branch that
can be driven should be driven.

The review found two more things in the same pass. The warning fired once per
SPAWN, which means once per pipeline stage, and that is the `xhci::report`
flood exactly: an unconditional print on a hot path, deleted once before for
drowning the Parallels console. It is once per environment change now, held in
a `Cell` on `Env` and cleared by `set`/`unset`, measured at one warning for a
two-stage pipeline, none for the next, and one again after the environment
changed. And the journal index still said six items for a row that now lists
seven, which nothing checks and so would have stayed wrong.

The thing worth keeping is not the bug. It is that I wrote a confident
reachability claim, used it to justify testing by mutation instead of for real,
and put it in the permanent record. The number was checkable in one line of
arithmetic and the ABI doc already contradicted it.

The day's pattern held to the end. Three code arcs and a documentation pass,
and the most valuable single result came from writing down what the code was
supposed to do and then checking. The syscall table was meant to be
housekeeping.

## Session-scoped authentication: measured before it was planned

The standup's one new item was "sign once per session rather than per verb".
Reading the auth spec in `ninep-abi` before planning anything showed that the
phrase, taken literally, describes a downgrade: verify one signature, then
trust a cleartext TCP connection, and every later request on it has no
integrity protection at all. The only version worth building agrees a session
key and MACs every message, which needs X25519 and HMAC, neither of which the
tree has any more.

The other half of the premise was cost, and the cost had been measured once, on
2026-08-31, at about 1.4 ms per sign-and-verify and judged small beside the
network. That comparison was made on one-shot connections, which also pay a TCP
connect per request. A held session does not, and nobody had measured it. So
the arc started with a measurement rather than a plan: a throwaway branch,
`measure/session-auth-cost` (`15a26dd`, pushed so the plan's citation resolves,
never to be merged), timing the four signature calls with `now_us()` into
relaxed atomic counters printed from the top of `serve`'s loop, because `log()`
carries 2 KB of buffers and the signature paths are the deepest frames in the
server.

`cbig` three times on the two-node ext2 rig gave 521, 943, 523 and 939 µs per
operation: **2,926 µs of crypto in every fid verb**, against a median verb of
6,654 µs (44%) and a mean of 11,196 µs (26%). The probe was checked against
something it shares no code with: `drive-2vm.py`'s count of `AUTHNP03` magics
in each node's capture said 42, the counters said 42, and the workload predicts
42 (three `NP_SESSION`s plus 13 verbs a run). The sign-plus-verify pair also
reproduced the 08-31 figure by a different tool on a different path. The plan,
[`roadmap-session-auth.md`](../roadmap/roadmap-session-auth.md), opens with
both results: why the literal reading is a downgrade, and the number that says
the arc is worth doing.

## Four reviews of a plan

The plan went through four review rounds before merging as #157, and the rounds
did not find the same kind of thing.

The first found the premise I had left out: the design needs a secret, fresh
X25519 ephemeral on each side, and Parallels and the Pi have no entropy at all.
Worse than guessable keys, an export ephemeral that repeats for a replayed
`NP_SESSION` hands the replay the same session key, which is the very replay
the plan claimed to close. Hans chose ephemerals derived from the machine key
and a persisted boot counter. The second round found that my derivation could
not be built: the random bytes mixed into the client's ephemeral contradicted
re-deriving it later, the nonce it hashed is created inside the signing call
after the payload that carries the key, and no step built the counter. Three
defects, all in the repair. Hans moved the counter into the kernel, through
UEFI before `ExitBootServices`, and the decision was rewritten whole rather
than patched a third time.

The third round's top finding was in that rewrite too: judging the counter
"persisted" by reading it back in the same boot cannot tell real storage from
edk2's RAM-only variables, which is exactly how every QEMU target here boots.
The fix is to trust only a counter that existed before this boot. The same
round found that step 7, the `netd` client that actually ships, had no control
at all, so a client accepting any reply would have passed. The fourth round, at
medium, found four smaller things, one of them again in the previous repair
(two counter stores could hand out the same value) and one a control listed
under step 6 that an honest export could never trigger. It merged after that.
The retrospective on the rounds is a dated section in
[`repairing-the-repairs-postmortem.md`](../postmortems/repairing-the-repairs-postmortem.md).
