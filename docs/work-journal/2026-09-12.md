## 2026-09-12: fid-verbs step 5, the export half, built and then hardened by two reviews

Five days after the 09-07 closeout, and the tree was exactly where that
standup left it: `main` at `932bcc0` plus its merge, clean, and the
`roadmap-fid-verbs.md` plan already written through step 7. So the day had a
named next move and took it: **step 5, the export half.** By its close the
export serves `NP_OPEN`, `NP_FSTAT` and `NP_CLUNK` on a session, across two
merged pull requests (#128, #129), and the reasoning below is mostly about the
things that are not in the diff: what the reviews found, which of their
findings were real, and the one they got backwards.

### What step 5 is, and the shape the plan already fixed

A *fid* is 9P's open-file handle, the server-side counterpart to a POSIX fd. A
client opens a path once, reads and writes through the returned handle, and
clunks it to close. `fsd` has implemented all five fid verbs since the libc
arc; the export (`netd`'s 9P-over-TCP gateway) implemented none, so a C
program's `open()` on a *remote* mount had nothing to answer it. Step 4
(09-07) built the piece that made an export fid possible at all: a **session**,
a connection held across requests, because a fid needs a lifetime and every
remote request before that opened its own connection and closed it. Step 5
hangs the handles off that session.

Two decisions from the plan did the load-bearing work before a line was
written. **Decision 2**, `netd` owns the fids: a remote client names `netd`'s
number (`FID_BASE + slot`), never `fsd`'s, because `fsd`'s ownership check is
keyed on the calling task slot and every export request arrives as the same
slot (`NET_TASK`), so `fsd` alone could not tell two remote clients apart. The
fid table therefore lives in `TcpConn`, four slots, each recording the `fsd`
fid behind it and the remote user who opened it. **Step 2's trap**, carried in
the plan in block capitals: `NP_OPEN`'s parameters are reversed from every
other path verb (`a0` is the open flags, `a1` the path length), so it cannot
go through the generic path decode. Both were known going in, which is why the
build itself was uneventful.

### The gate, and why a mutation is the real test

The export half went in behind a new `np9p_client.py fid-gate`: open, fstat,
clunk over one session, the `fstat` record byte-compared against `NP_STAT`'s
for the same path, then the three negative controls the plan named (a fid
never opened is refused; a fid from session A is refused on session B; closing
a session leaves its numbers dead on the next). Run under `run-guest.sh`, not
`drive-qemu.py`, because the blind-instruments lesson from 09-07 is still the
freshest scar: a host client outliving the guest reads a killed process as a
refusing one. Against `main` first, to watch it fail: 8 of 10, the two
boundary pins passing by construction. Against the branch: 10 of 10, guest
alive, zero aborts.

The check that matters most is the one a per-connection table cannot pass on
its own. Closing a session must clunk its fids *on `fsd`*, or `fsd`'s own
eight-slot table leaks until it restarts. A per-connection table refuses a
stale number whether or not `fsd` was ever told, so "an old number is dead on
a fresh session" proves nothing about `fsd`. The gate adds the half that can
fail: twelve opens across three closed sessions against `fsd`'s eight slots,
which succeed only if each close really clunked. Two mutations confirmed the
checks bite: remove the `Drop` that clunks the table and the twelve-opens
check fails at the eighth; remove the per-user check and `fsd` drops the
co-tenant's fid, so the owner's next `fstat` fails too. A gate whose checks
have not each been watched to fail is a gate that proves nothing, and that was
the whole of the 09-07 finding.

### The high review, and which of ten findings were real

The `high` review returned ten findings. Six were worth fixing on the spot and
went into #128 alongside the feature: the never-opened control was probing the
same fid it had just clunked (identical to the check above it, could not fail
independently); an unserved verb logged `one-shot` where a reader greps for
`export`; `FS_ERR_NO_SUCH_VERB` now carries a second meaning ("not on this
kind of connection") that its doc and the client messages did not mention;
`Drop`'s doc read as the connection's whole teardown when a running `cpu`
child is not released there; the plan's claim that Decision 2 "makes 8 an
`fsd`-local number again" was false, since every remote fid is also an `fsd`
fid. Small, all of them, and all the same shape: a claim that went stale the
moment the code around it moved.

Three more were the real defects, and all three are the same underlying fact
seen from different angles: **an `fsd` fid can outlive what `netd` knows about
it.** A `netd` restart leaves `fsd` holding fids owned by a `NET_TASK` slot
that is alive again the instant the fresh `netd` loads, so `fsd` never reaps
them. An `fsd` restart wipes its table and reissues numbers from three, so a
session's cached number now names a different file. And `fsd`'s ownership rule,
written for a sender that is never recycled, treats a uid mismatch as a
recycled slot and drops the fid. The first and third are an `fsd`-side change
(the slot-as-identity ledger item), deferred to their own PR. The second,
`fsd`-restart aliasing, went into #129: a `SessionFid` now records
`TASK_IDENTITY(FSD_TASK)` at open, and every op and `Drop` compares it against
`fsd`'s current generation, treating a mismatch as a dead handle.

### The item the reviews disagreed about, and the race with a safe direction

Two things are worth carrying past this arc.

The first is **item 9, the refactor I chose not to do.** Folding the fid
dispatch into `build_9p_reply` is a real deduplication, but its payoff is step
6's: `NP_PREAD` would become one arm beside `NP_READ_AT`, reusing the chunk
machinery that lives only in `build_9p_reply`. Doing it now means touching the
dispatch preamble every path verb runs through, which the fid gate does not
exercise, for no behavioural gain today. Hans asked which choice fit the
standing policy, stable before secure before open-to-expansion, and the
answer was the same on all three axes: defer. It is safest (the blast radius
exceeds the test coverage here), neutral on security, and *better* for future
expansion, because step 6 brings both the consumer that justifies the fold and
the read-path gate that would cover it. The merge-with-consumer lesson from
`cluster-phase0-postmortem.md`, landed against. Recorded at step 6 in the
plan so it is not re-litigated.

The second is **a race whose obvious fix reintroduces the bug.** The `low`
review, run over #129, flagged the generation guard: `netd` reads `fsd`'s
generation *before* the open call and records that, and the review called it a
latent bug, suggesting the read move to after the open. It should not. There
is no atomic "open and report the generation", so an `fsd` restart in the
window is recorded wrong whichever side the read lands, and the two directions
are not equal. Reading before records the old generation against a fid on the
new one, so a later op sees a mismatch and refuses a *live* fid: the client
re-opens, harmless. Reading after would record the new generation against a
fid opened on the old, now-wiped `fsd`, so a later op sees a match and forwards
a stale number `fsd` may have reissued, which is the exact aliasing the guard
exists to stop. The guard must fail toward false-dead, never false-live. The
ordering stayed; a comment now records why, so the next reviewer does not make
the swap. A well-meant "obvious fix" that quietly undoes the defence is its own
small hazard, and the defence against it is the same as always: say, in the
code, why the non-obvious thing is the right one.

### The afternoon: the `fsd`-side change, one mechanism for two leaks

Review items 10 and 11 from the morning were queued as one `fsd`-side PR, and
the standup called them the easiest of the four open items. They were, and
they turned out to be one change. `fsd`'s `Fid.owner` was a task slot, and
its own doc said so; the roadmap's slot-as-identity ledger had already named
it as the next thing to convert after `netd`'s remote-exec child. Making it
the packed `SENDER_TASK` identity, slot and generation captured at send time,
closes both items at once. The ownership test for all four fid verbs,
`NP_CLUNK` included, is now `(owner, owner_uid)` against the sender's identity
and the caller's uid, and a uid mismatch is refused and the fid *kept*: the old
drop existed only because a uid change was the one way a slot-keyed owner
could notice a recycled slot, and on a session it let a co-tenant destroy a
handle by naming it (item 10). And the reaper frees by identity, comparing
the slot's current `TASK_IDENTITY` against the one recorded at open, so a
restarted `netd`, a new generation in the same protected slot, has its old
fids reclaimed at the next full table rather than for the boot (item 11).

The reaper change fixed a third bug nobody had listed. The shell runs every
foreground command in the same slot, so eight C programs that each leaked
one fid left the ninth, alive in that very slot, unable to open anything;
`TASK_STATE` reported the slot alive and freed nothing. `libc/src/file.c`'s
own comment on `__libc_close_all` had described exactly this failure as the
reason it closes at exit, and the reason was true: the libc's close-all was
the only thing standing between a leaking program and a stuck table.

**The check is a program that leaks on purpose.** The condition cannot be
made from the shell, so `libc/cleak.c` (`/bin/CLEAK`) opens a file and leaves
through the raw `EXIT` syscall, past `_exit`'s close-all. Nine runs in a row
under `drive-qemu.py`: on the branch all nine open and `cfile` works after;
against `main`'s `fsd`, built into the same image for one run, the ninth
prints `open failed` and so does `cfile`. Watched to fail, then to pass. The
second control reused the morning's mutation the other way round: remove
`netd`'s per-user fid check and run the gate. In the morning that made `fsd`
drop the owner's fid and the co-tenant check failed; now `fsd` refuses the
co-tenant and keeps the fid, and the gate passes on `fsd`'s wall alone.
The gate unmutated passes too, guest alive, 0 restarts, 0 aborts;
`make test` green, `check-relocs` 63 binaries, 0 ABS64.

What the run does not exercise is a real `netd` restart. The rig for one
exists (the `wedgeme` mutation in `testing-qemu.md`), but showing the leak
needs two sessions holding eight fids across the restart and then a third
opening, and that is a host-side sequence the gate does not have. The
mechanism is the identity comparison the nine-run check does exercise, and
the kernel issues a fresh generation on `install_task`; that is stated in the
testing doc as the boundary of what was measured, not folded into the claim.

Comments that had become false were brought up to date in the same commit:
`netd`'s `SessionFid::opener` no longer says `fsd` drops, and the libc's
close-all comment no longer says a leaked fid is permanent. Both were true
when written.

**The high review of #130 returned ten findings, and seven were right.** The
best of them was the one the morning's lesson predicted: the new `NP_CLUNK`
ownership test had no observer. The gate's co-tenant probe was an fstat,
which `netd` refuses before `fsd` sees it; the host peer freed any known fid
for any caller; the wire spec still said a clunk answers 0. So the uid test
on the freeing verb could have been reverted with every check green. The fix
is a check in each place: the spec says ownership is tested on clunk as on
every fid verb, the host peer binds a fid to the requesting user, and the
gate gained an eleventh check, another user's clunk refused and the owner
still holding then closing the fid. Watched to fail the right way: with
`netd`'s per-user check removed and `fsd` made to skip the uid test on clunk,
that one check fails and the fstat check beside it still passes.

The other real ones: `NP_OPEN` truncated or created the file BEFORE finding
out the table was full, so a refused open could empty a file the caller then
held no handle to (pre-existing, and the PR's own `cleak` can fill the table
to show it; the slot is now taken before the side effects). The uid half of
the ownership test was applied to every owner, though it only carries
information for `netd`; for a local task the identity is already unique, and
it would have stranded a fid across a POSIX privilege drop, so it applies to
`NET_TASK` only. The reaper's two syscalls had a window between them; one
read has none. The `GET_ID_ERR` guard on open could not fail, and its comment
described the inverse of what would have happened if it could; gone. `netd`'s
clunk arm cleared its slot whatever `fsd` answered, which with `fsd` now
keeping the fid on `FS_ERR_PERM` would have orphaned it under `netd`'s
unreapable identity. And the two functions took a slot and an identity as
adjacent `u64`s behind two clippy allows; they take the identity and derive
the slot now, so the swap is unspellable.

Two declined, with the reason at the site. The gen-before-open window can
leak one fid on `fsd` per fsd-restart-during-open; clunking on mismatch
would reintroduce the aliasing the guard exists to stop, so the cost is
stated rather than paid. And freeing a predecessor's fids by comparing
generations within one slot is exact and free, but it would let the nine-run
check pass with the full-table reaper broken, and a check that can fail is
worth more than reclaiming a leaked slot a few requests earlier.

One process scar from the controls: reverting a mutation with `git checkout`
restores the COMMITTED file, and the review fixes were not yet committed, so
the restore took them with it. The measurements stood (the mutations were
applied on top of the fixes), the edits were reapplied from the script that
made them, and the gate was rerun on the committed tree. Commit before
mutating; the testing doc now says so.

## 2026-09-12: fid-verbs step 6, `NP_PREAD`, and the fold that came with it

The third piece of the day, after the standup named it first: the export
serves `NP_PREAD` on a session, and the fid verbs stopped living in their own
function. Branch `netd/fid-verbs-step6`.

### The plan was wrong about the mechanism, and right about the reuse

Step 6's sentence said "the wire→`SAFECOPY` bridge, mirroring
`read_file_chunk`". Reading `fsd`'s arm rather than the verb's name: `fsd`'s
`NP_PREAD` answers *inline*, the bytes in its reply like `NP_READDIR`'s, not
through a grant like the path-based `NP_READ`. So there is no bridge to build
and `read_file_chunk` is reused by nothing. What is reused is `fsd_call3`,
`ok_data` and the readdir arm's `DATA_INLINE` cap, which is the claim the
plan actually cared about: one chunk cap, not a second copy. The correction
is recorded in the step, not smoothed over.

### The fold

`fid_verb_reply` re-spelled `build_9p_reply`'s preamble a second way (the
runt check, the header decode, the `EXPORT_NS` resolve, the non-`Fsd`
refusal), which the review of #128 had asked to fold and the plan had tied to
this step, since the fold touches the preamble every path verb runs through
and only a step with a consumer and a gate should touch that. `build_9p_reply`
now takes the connection's fid table and whether it is a session;
`path_len_word` says which request word carries the path length per verb
(`a0`; `a1` for `NP_OPEN`, whose `a0` is the flags; none for the ops on a fid,
which skip the namespace rather than resolve a payload they do not have);
`session_slot` is the shared front half of the three ops on a fid, with the
three refusals in the order the review of #130 fixed; and the four served fid
verbs are arms of the one match, `NP_PREAD` beside `NP_READ_AT`. `NP_PWRITE`
falls to the catch-all on any connection, logged `export: no arm`, so the log
still names the verb that is missing rather than the connection kind.

One consequence worth a line: an `NP_OPEN` of `/dev/cons` or `/net/ip` now
lands in that target's own arm and is refused there, logged as that target's
missing arm, where the old function refused it before resolution with a
generic line. Same code, better log, and the path gate pins it.

### Two gates, four controls

The fid gate grew four checks and is 14 of 14: a `pread` of a never-opened fid
refused; another user's `pread` refused `FS_ERR_PERM` with the owner's next
read served; `/man/grep` (4661 bytes, nine chunks and a tail) read to EOF
through a fid and byte-equal to the same file over `NP_READ_AT` on one-shot
connections; EOF answering 0 with the session in phase. The compare fails on a
file that fits one chunk, on purpose: a same-length compare that passes on a
short read is the failure mode, and a one-chunk file cannot show it.

The fold needed a gate of its own, since the fid gate exercises none of
`write`/`read_at`/`write_at`/`mv`/`chmod`/`touch`/`rm`/`mkdir`. `path-gate`
runs each end to end on a scratch file, plus the console and `/net` arms, and
the `NP_OPEN`-of-a-non-`fsd`-target refusal on a session: 12 of 12. Nine
client constants went into the wire checker for it (102 now agree, the
client's baseline 40, counted).

Four mutation runs, the tree committed before each, `git checkout` after:
against `main`'s export the four new checks fail with `FS_ERR_NO_SUCH_VERB`
and the ten step-5 checks pass; the expected buffer one byte short fails the
compare alone (4661 against 4660); the export forwarding `offset + 1` fails
the compare alone the other way (4660 against 4661); and the selector reading
`a1` for every path verb fails the path gate 10 of 12. The two survivors of
that last run are the honest part: `read_at` with `a1` = 100 and `chmod` with
`a1` = 0o600 pass because a too-long length is clamped to the whole payload,
which for a path-only request *is* the path. The clamp is right, it is what
keeps a runt frame from panicking `netd`, and it means a wrong word hides
whenever the wrong number is larger than the right one. The gate's other ten
are the ones that see it, and the doc says so rather than quoting 12 of 12.

A short-read mutation was considered and rejected as a control: capping each
chunk at 511 delivers the whole file anyway, because a short read is legal and
the client continues from it. A control that the correct behaviour passes is
not a control.
