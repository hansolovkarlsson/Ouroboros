## Which chain: the reading was not the deepest

The open question from the stack work was which call chain leaves `netd`
2,528 bytes. I read it off the binary: each function's frame from the stack
pointer's decrements in its disassembly, summed down every direct call chain
from `_start`.

The first reading said the deepest chain was 19 KB, against a stack the
kernel's mark showed 52 KB deep at the prompt. The script was wrong, and it
was the measurement that said so. A frame over a page is allocated by a
probe loop, `sub x9, sp, #N, lsl #12` and then a page at a time down to it,
and I had counted the loop's one page. With the loop read, `serve`'s frame
is 43,360 bytes, and `main`, `serve`, `drain_client_messages` and
`handle_client` come to 52,064: exactly the 5,280 left that
`/proc/4/stackfree` reads after boot.

Below `handle_client` the chain the rigs had been reading is the remote
mount naming its caller from `/etc/passwd`. It is not the deepest.
`resolve_ip` into `arp_resolve` leaves 1,168 bytes. I booted an image with
a NIC and typed `resolve example.com`, and the mark went from 5,280 to
1,168, the figure to the byte. No rig resolves a name. For two days the
records had said "at most 2,528", and the wording was careful about one
limit, that the mark counts writes and not the stack pointer, and silent
about the other: a mark covers only the paths something drove.

`netd` also turned out to scrub its own stack after a user signature, 6 KB
of zeros below the caller. I checked whether that was writing the low mark,
and it is not: those chains sit about 5 KB above the guard.

## The reading as a build check (#441)

Hans chose to fix the frame and to have the check first. `make
check-stack-chains` runs the reading over all 103 userland binaries and
fails a program whose deepest chain leaves under 4,096 bytes. Three things
shaped it.

A recursive program has no deepest chain. The first cycle detection was
order dependent and reported different cycles on different runs; it is
Tarjan's components now. A program with a cycle must be named in the script,
with the number of times the cycle can be walked if the source bounds it
(`fsd`'s `free_pointed`, twice) or as bounded only by its input. Twelve are
the second kind. `tree` is one: 1,312 bytes a directory level, so about
forty levels deep is its guard page. That is a defect the check found and I
did not fix.

C's static functions share names, and keying functions by name merged two
`open`s into one function with a cycle that was not there. Functions are
keyed by address.

And `netd` could not pass a floor of 4,096 with 1,168 left, so it got an
entry letting it stand at 1,024, built so that an entry which comes to keep
the floor fails the run. That fired the same morning, as intended.

The review of the script found the reading had been blind in the binaries
DevTools's linker makes, which keep no symbols for static functions: a call
to one reads as `bl <stat+0x410>`, and I had dropped every call whose target
was not a symbol. Half the calls in the four `-ld` variants led nowhere,
and two editor builds read as shallow and not recursive. Any address a `bl`
names is now a function start. Rewriting the parser for that, I broke the
pattern that matches an instruction line (the disassembler pads with spaces
before the tab; my hand-written self-test did not). The self-test passed.
The canary did not: it asserts that `stackprobe`'s `dig` frame reads as the
1,072 bytes the stack rig measures, and every real binary was reading as
empty. The self-test's text is now spelled as the disassembler spells it.

## The tables off the stack (#442)

`serve`'s locals were everything `netd` keeps for the whole boot: two
receive buffers, the auth config, four tables, the pending run, the held
keys. They were on the stack because userland has no mutable statics, the
linker script asserting `.data` and `.bss` empty. I had written "in
statics" on the roadmap before remembering that. `netd` does have a heap
region, a megabyte of it, with 8 KiB in use for `cpu`'s read buffer. The
tables are one struct there, past that buffer, built once before the loop.
`serve`'s body changed only in reaching each table through the reference,
and no callee's signature changed.

The deepest chain leaves 37,296 bytes. The boot measurement agrees to the
byte again, after `resolve example.com`.

Two of my claims did not survive being run. I wrote in a comment that the
constructor must never be inlined into `serve` or the whole 43 KB would be
back. Forcing the inline cost 1,632 bytes, the compiler building most of
the struct in place; the comment says that now. And the stack rig's size
check, the one made to assert its premise that morning, failed: `netd`'s
count had grown a digit, so the two files no longer differed in length. It
failed on the premise and said so, which is what the assertion was for. It
compares two probes of known depth now.

The review found nothing wrong in the move and ten comments that still
sized a table against `serve`'s stack, one of them about where session
keys sit after a close. The table sizes were capped by the stack and now
cost heap; I left them as they were and said so at each.

## `tree`, and a defect that was the check's own caveat (#444)

I recommended the repair before reading the program, and then read it. The
standup said `tree` recurses 1,312 bytes a level, so a tree about forty
levels deep reaches its guard page, repair by an explicit stack. `tree` has
capped its walk at sixteen levels since it was written. The stack check
follows calls and cannot see a bound in a variable, so its note for `tree`
said what an unbounded recursion would do, and I had copied the note into
the roadmap as a finding the day before. I told Hans the item was wrong
before building anything, and what I proposed instead was smaller: the two
constants the cap is computed from were stale (1,024 a level and 8 KiB kept
back, against 1,312 and 8,240 in the binary), harmless on fourteen stack
pages and wrong on about seven, and the cut at the cap was silent.

The boot that was meant to show the new "not descended" line showed
something I did not know. `mkdir` refused the seventeenth level with "path
too long": a path holds sixteen names, so from any root but `/` the walk is
stopped by the path limit before the depth cap, and that stop was silent
too. It got its own line. A directory deep enough to show either had to be
made on the host with `debugfs`, since no command here can name one. The
check now reads `tree`'s three constants from its source, counts `walk`
sixteen times, and fails when a constant falls under the binary; each of
three mutations failed it.

## The refused SD write (#445)

Hans had been promised the options before any building, and took the third:
`fsd` asks the kernel about a partition as it mounts it and holds one the
driver never writes read-only, and the kernel also answers a declined write
apart from a failed one. I had counted two layers that flattened the
refusal into an I/O error. There were three: the shell's `erase`,
`partition` and `format` each print one line for every failure.

`format` did not change its message when the other two did, and that was the
day's second false record. The rig's "format over the firmware partition
refused" had never reached the guard: `format` targets the root, which is
too small for FAT32, and is refused by arithmetic. A tenth boot now puts
the firmware partition where `format` goes.

I had decided two things without Hans, and the review reversed one. I left
the write-protect switch to the second line only, on the grounds that it is
read at each write and a mount-time answer could go stale. The review's
point was plainer: a switched card then mounts with no word, which is the
symptom the change exists to remove. It is asked at the mount now. I tried
to grade it with a read-only drive and QEMU refuses one as an SD card, so
the switch has no check. The review also had `fsd` ask about the extent the
volume mounted with and not the table's length, made the question part of
the one constructor a disk mount has, and found the message blaming the
firmware partition for refusals the switch makes.

`test-boot-partition` failed once that afternoon, in a USB-booted scenario
that stopped at the password prompt with no fault line, and passed on the
rerun and on a later run. I did not find why. It reads as a lost keystroke;
it is not shown to be one.
