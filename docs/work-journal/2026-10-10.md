# 2026-10-10

## #253 merged, and the `Ozq` check made to fail (#254)

#253 merged in the morning after a low review found nothing. Hans then asked
for the check it had left blind. The question was why `test-kbd-mode`'s Esc
check could not fail even with E1 and E2 both removed, which a reading of the
queue said it should. The transcript of that run answered it: between the
interrupt and `Ozq` the rig typed the shell's next command, `readkey spin 100
keep`, and under the day-old `keyseq` its `r` ends any open Esc. The bytes
the check meant to probe were never the first after the interrupt. On main
the same check had been masked by E2 instead, which closes the Esc 40 ms
after it is read, long before the next key is.

The fix was to make `Ozq` the shell's next line. Run three ways: green on the
tree, red with E1 and E2 removed together (the shell ran `q`), green with E1
alone removed. That last result is not a weakness of the check but a fact
about the system: E1 matters only for a key read within E2's interval of the
Esc, and the shell's wake and its `wait: interrupted` line take longer, so no
rig can see E1 alone. Its host test is the check for it, which
`test-kbd-cut`'s check 3 already said for the kill path.

## What the session was made of

Three PRs across the evening of 10-09 and the morning of 10-10, each merged on
Hans's word. The largest, #253, went through two high reviews and a low one,
and its design changed between them: a cost I had called acceptable looked
different once a reviewer spelled it out as a login that fails. The lesson I
would keep is about controls: a change that widens what the system accepts
silently blinds every check whose control relied on the old refusal, and the
remedy is to choose the input the new rule still treats specially.
